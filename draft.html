Three ways to open the landing page

You asked whether there are other options for the words. There are, but two things are worth settling before picking one, because they are true of the page as it stands regardless of which direction wins.

1. The page promises matching in five places, and there is no matcher

This is the same thing as “Explore matches” on My partners, but stated far more strongly — and two of the five are machine-readable, so it is what search engines have been told PeerFlow does.

  • index.html:10 — meta description: “Match by path, level, goals and weekly availability.”
  • index.html:53 — JSON-LD: “PeerFlow matches tech learners across eight learning paths…”
  • index.html:164 — the hero: “Match with one learner on the same path and level.”
  • index.html:454 — paths: “PeerFlow matches you with someone learning in the same direction.”
  • index.html:643 — “Eight paths, one format: matched peer-to-peer study…”

What actually happens: you open a list of everyone, sorted so the closest person is first, and you ask them yourself. That is a good product. It is not matching, and somebody who arrives expecting to be matched is disappointed in the first thirty seconds — before they have met anybody.

2. The best sentence on the page is the last one on the page

“I grew up in a small town. I didn’t need another course — I needed someone who’d show up on Tuesday because I was expecting them.”

That is the product, in one line, in a voice no competitor can copy. It is currently below the guides, below the eight path cards, below the four-step explainer — roughly 4,000 pixels down, where almost nobody reaches it. The hero instead says “Find your tech study partner”, which names the category rather than the thing.

Option A

Lead with the line that is already at the bottom

Move the founder’s insight to the top and let the mechanism follow it. The strongest option if the page has one job: making somebody feel the difference between a course and a person. Risk: it is a feeling rather than a description, so the subhead has to work harder.

Someone is expecting you on Tuesday.

That is the whole difference. One study partner, the same hour every week, cameras on. Not another course you start alone and stop alone.

Section 2 becomes

“Why people stop.” One sentence, not three bullets: most people quit in the weeks when nothing is due and nobody notices.

The paths section

“Eight paths. Pick yours and see who is already on it.” — a directory, honestly named.

The founder quote

Stays at the foot, now as the proof of the headline rather than as the reveal.

Option B

Say the mechanism, plainly

No feeling, no promise, no abstraction — just what you get, in words nobody has to decode. The safest option, and the most defensible: every claim on it is one the product keeps. Risk: it is calm to the point of quiet, and a quiet headline has to be carried by the page under it.

One partner.
One hour a week.
Cameras on.

Pick your path, find someone learning the same thing at roughly your level, and agree a regular time. You both turn up, or you both know about it.

See who is here

Eight tech paths · one-to-one · 50 minutes a week · free

Section 2 becomes

“What an hour looks like.” The 50-minute shape, which is the thing people are most unsure about and the page never shows.

The paths section

Unchanged apart from the word “matches”, which becomes “shows you who else is on it”.

The founder quote

Moves up, directly under the hero, as the reason the mechanism is shaped this way.

Option C

Name the failure first

Open on the thing the reader has already lived, then offer the fix. This is what the current page attempts with “Sounds familiar?” and three bullets — the same idea, done in one specific sentence instead of three generic ones. Risk: opening on failure is a tone some people bounce off.

You didn’t run out of courses.

You ran out of reasons to open one on a Tuesday night. PeerFlow gives you a person instead — same path, same level, same hour every week, on camera.

Section 2 becomes

The current three bullets, kept — they now answer the headline instead of repeating it.

The paths section

“Eight paths. Pick yours and see who is already on it.”

The founder quote

Stays at the foot. The headline already borrows its argument, so leading with it as well would say it twice.

What I would pick, and what I would do either way

B for the hero, A’s ending. The product's whole claim is that it is honest about being small and real — no algorithm, no course library, no gamification — and B is the only one of the three whose headline is a fact you can check. A has the better emotional line, which is why it should stay where it lands hardest rather than being spent at the top.

Regardless of which wins: the five “match” claims should go. That one is not a matter of taste — the page is describing a feature that does not exist, in the two places search engines read, and it sets up every new arrival to be disappointed by a product that would otherwise have pleased them.
