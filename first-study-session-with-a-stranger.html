Your first study session with a stranger

Almost everything written about study partners is about finding one. Very little is about the ten minutes after they appear on screen, which is where most partnerships are actually decided.

6 min readWhat to say, in what order, and the four ways it goes wrong

You agreed a time with somebody you have never met. The hour arrives. Their camera comes on, yours comes on, and there are two people looking at each other with nothing agreed about what happens next.

That moment is not a social problem. It is a logistical one, and it has a short answer: three things, in order, in about six minutes. Everything below is that.

Why the first ten minutes decide it

A study partnership fails in one of two ways. The dramatic one is that somebody stops turning up, and everyone assumes that was the problem. It usually is not — it is the symptom.

The quiet one is that the first session produces nothing, both people privately conclude this does not work, and neither says so. There is no argument and no cancellation. The second session is just harder to book than the first, the third never gets proposed, and a month later it has ended without anybody deciding to end it.

What produces nothing is almost never the wrong subject or the wrong level. It is one person expecting fifty quiet minutes beside somebody, and the other expecting a conversation. Both are reasonable. Neither is guessable.

The three things, in order

1. Say what you are doing today — not what you are learning

"I'm learning React" is a biography. "Today I'm trying to get a form to stop re-rendering on every keystroke" is a thing that can happen in the next fifty minutes.

Keep it to about thirty seconds each. The purpose is not to get to know each other; it is so that at the end, both of you can say whether it happened.

2. Agree whether this hour has talking in it

This is the one that decides everything and the one nobody asks. There are two kinds of session and they are both good:

  • Quiet. Cameras on, microphones off or silent, both working. You are there so that leaving is awkward. That is the entire mechanism and it works.
  • Talking. You interrupt each other, ask questions, screen-share when stuck. Slower, and much better when one of you is blocked on something the other has seen before.

Pick one out loud, now, for this session only. You can pick the other one next week.

3. Say when you are stopping

"Let's go until twenty to" is a complete sentence and it removes the worst part of the ending, which is two people wondering who is going to say it first. Fifty minutes is the default on PeerFlow because it leaves ten minutes on the hour for the part below.

The lines, if you want them

There is nothing clever here. The value is that somebody said them rather than waiting.

Opening Hi — I'm Munisa. Today I'm trying to finish the auth flow on a side project. What are you on?
The one that matters Do you want this to be a quiet one, or shall we talk as we go? I'm happy either way — quiet works better for me when I already know what I'm doing.
Closing the opening Let's go until twenty to, then take five minutes at the end to say where we got.
If they are stuck and you can help Do you want to share your screen for a minute? No pressure — I'm equally happy to leave you to it.

Four things that go wrong

Filling the silence

Thirty seconds of nobody talking feels like a failure and is not one. In a quiet session that is the session working. Resist narrating what you are doing to prove you are still there — somebody who has to perform concentration is not concentrating.

Treating it as an interview

Asking where somebody works, how long they have been coding, what they want to do next: all reasonable, all better in week three. In the first session it costs the time you came for and raises the stakes of an hour that should be low-stakes.

Teaching when you were not asked

If you know more about their problem than they do, the temptation is enormous and it changes what the partnership is. Two learners beside each other is a different arrangement from a lesson, and the second one ends faster — the person being taught starts to feel they are taking, and stops booking.

Apologising for your setup

Your room, your accent, your camera, the noise outside. Nobody has ever left a study partnership over any of these, and leading with an apology makes them the subject of the first minute.

The last five minutes

Stop when you said you would, and spend the end on two questions:

  • Did the thing you named at the start happen? Say so plainly either way. "I didn't get there, I got stuck on the redirect" is a better answer than a summary, and it gives next week a starting point.
  • Same time next week? Ask it in the session, not in a message afterwards. A partnership that has to be re-negotiated by text every week is one that quietly ends.

On PeerFlow this is one control — a standing slot books the same hour every week from then on, so the second session is agreed while the first one is still fresh rather than by somebody remembering to ask.

If it is not going to work

Sometimes it is clear in the first session that you are not compatible: wildly different levels, or one of you wants a tutor and the other wants company. That is worth saying, and it is kinder said early.

"I think we're after quite different things from this — I don't want to waste your Tuesdays" is a complete and honest end, and both of you can go and find somebody better suited the same week. The version where you stop replying takes a month and leaves the other person guessing.

And if something is wrong rather than merely unsuited — somebody who makes you uncomfortable, or who will not respect what you said about how the hour works — that is a different thing entirely, and every PeerFlow session has report and block on it. What those do, and what you control is worth reading once before your first session.

The short version

  • Say what you are doing today, in one sentence.
  • Ask out loud whether this is a quiet hour or a talking one.
  • Name the time you will stop.
  • At the end: did it happen, and same time next week.

That is six minutes of talking and it is the difference between a first session and a partnership.
