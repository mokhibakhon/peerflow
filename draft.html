What to ask after a session, and what the rail is for

Two decisions rather than clean-ups. First: you were right that asking people whether they turned up is the wrong question — the room should know, and we have not built a room. So the question that is actually left is what you meant to get done, which the schema has columns for and nobody is asked. Second: the rail is four cards deep and offers a power feature to people who have not had a session yet. Nothing here is live.

One — the question worth asking after a session

Now

We cannot see whether anybody turned up, and asking was the wrong fix

There was a version of this draft that offered three places to ask "did Friday happen?". That was wrong and it is gone. Attendance is not something to ask a person — it is something the room knows. The reason we do not know it is that there is no room: the call is a link to a public Jitsi address opened in another tab, so PeerFlow sees nothing at all — not who joined, not when, not for how long.

The schema already assumed the room would tell us. migration-reliability.sql scores a session partly on how late somebody joined: 

when s.attended is true and s.joined_at is not null
     and s.joined_at > s.starts_at + interval '10 minutes' then 0.8

joined_at is written by nothing, and can only ever be written by the call itself. So the reliability score stays a dash until the call is ours — and a self-reported number in its place would be worse than the dash, because it renders as a percentage on the People page that strangers use to decide whether to partner with you.

So: attendance waits for the video work. Join and leave events from the call fill attended and joined_at, and nobody is ever asked. What follows is the question that is left — the one no server can answer.

The gap

A session has a topic and nothing else

Today a session carries a free-text topic typed once when it was booked — "Linux privesc" — and that is the whole record of what the two of you did. sessions.goal and sessions.goal_done exist in the schema and are empty. Whether you did the thing you sat down to do is the one fact about a session that a machine cannot observe, and it is the one nobody is asked.

A goal only works as a pair: declared before, answered after. Asked only afterwards it becomes a diary entry nobody writes. So the three options below differ in when it is declared — the answering half is the same short row in every one.

Option A

Declared when you book it

One more line in the booking form, beside the note you can already leave. Your partner sees it when they accept, so they turn up knowing what the hour is for.

Propose

Tuesday at 7:00 PM

Nothing is booked until they accept.

Trade-off. It costs nothing to build — the form is already there — and it makes the proposal more persuasive to accept, because it says what the hour is for rather than just when it is. But it is asked at the point furthest from the session, sometimes a week out, when you genuinely may not know yet. Expect a lot of blanks and a few that no longer match by the time Tuesday arrives.

Option B

Declared when the room opens

The band already changes fifteen minutes before a session to "your room is open". It asks there, in the one moment you are certainly thinking about this session, and the answer rides along into the call.

Your room is open

Asalxon is waiting.

50 minutes · Linux privesc

Join the call

Trade-off. Asked at the moment it is easiest to answer and hardest to forget, and it is the one point where both of you are present, so the two goals could be shown to each other. The cost is that it stands between somebody and a call that has already started — which is exactly when nobody wants a form. It has to be skippable in one tap, and if it is skippable it will often be skipped.

Option C

No goal — just say what you got done

Drop the before-half entirely. After the session, one line, free text, and it becomes the session's record on the partner page and on Progress.

Friday with Asalxon

Trade-off. Nothing stands between you and a call, and a written record of twelve sessions is genuinely worth having — it is the thing you would show somebody. But without a goal declared first there is nothing to compare it against, so it can never answer "did you do what you planned", only "what happened". That is a journal, not a loop, and journals are the feature people stop filling in second.

Built

A is live — B when the call is ours

A is no longer a proposal. The booking form asks for the goal, it rides with the proposal so whoever accepts can see what the hour is for, and after the session a row prints the goal back and asks whether you got it done — only ever for a session that had one written, so nobody who left the box empty is asked anything. The rest of this section is the reasoning, kept because it is why B is not built yet.

A costs one input in a form that already exists and improves the thing it sits in — a proposal that says what the hour is for is easier to accept than one that only says when. Take it now.

B is the better moment and the wrong time to build it. Once the call is in-app there is a room to put it in — a goal on the way in and the check on the way out, both inside the session rather than bolted to the dashboard around it. Building it into the band first means building it twice.

C I would not do on its own. As the second half of A it is the same row with the goal printed above the box, which is where it belongs.

Two — the streak card, and what the rail is for

Now

The number and the ring are about different things

The circle is not messy because it is a circle. It is messy because the 42px number in the middle is your streak in weeks, and the arc around it is this week against your goal — two measures at two speeds, sharing one shape. A big 3 inside a half-filled ring reads as "three out of something", and the something is never named. The comment in app.css says so outright and calls it the point: 

The number is weeks; the ring is this week against the goal.
Two clocks at two speeds in one object, which is the point.

There is a third telling of the same story underneath it — the twelve bars — so the card says "how am I doing" three times in three shapes. Every option below removes at least one of them.

And the thing I missed

The streak is already in the top bar

appshell.js draws a flame and the number in the chrome of every page in the app — same three states as the ring, so the bar and the card were built to agree with each other. Which means the 42px number in the middle of the ring is the second time you read your streak on the same screen, and the first one never scrolls away.

So the card does not need to say it at all. What the chrome cannot show is the two things the card is actually for: where this week stands, and what the run looks like. Both options below drop the number and keep those.

The earlier drafts also drew the twelve weeks as twelve identical bars with different fills, which is why they still looked odd — a chart whose height means nothing is not a chart. Height is the number of sessions now: full column, half column, or a stub.

This week, and the run — no ring, no number

The headline is the week you are in, because that is the only part you can still do something about. Underneath, twelve weeks as a real column chart. The streak stays in the top bar where it already is.

Trade-off. Nothing on the card repeats the chrome, the one graphic left is a real chart, and the biggest type is the number you can still change. The streak is still on screen — three inches up and to the right, on a flame, permanently. The cost is that the card is quieter than it was, and if you think the streak deserves to be the loudest thing on the dashboard then it argues the other way.

No card — the chrome has the streak, Progress has the rest

The rail loses it entirely. The flame in the bar carries the streak, the twelve weeks live on the Progress page the card was linking to anyway, and the dashboard goes back to being only about what to do next. The nudge — don’t leave Asalxon hanging — moves into the band, which is the element that already exists to say exactly that.

Trade-off. The shortest dashboard of anything drawn so far, and the nudge lands in the loudest place on the page instead of the third card down the rail. But the run of weeks stops being glanceable — you have to go to Progress to see it — and a streak you never see is a streak you stop minding, which is most of what it is for.

If you want my opinion

A

B is tempting and I think it goes one step too far: the twelve weeks are the only place on the dashboard you can see a gap, and moving them behind a click means nobody looks. A keeps them, drops everything that was being said twice, and leaves the card with one number and one chart that are about two different things and look it.

A is roughly forty lines out and fifteen in — the ring markup, its SVG, the segment maths and about thirty lines of CSS go; the bars gain a height class. momentum() already returns the count per week, so nothing behind it changes.

Also

Four cards, and one of them is offered too early

The rail runs about a thousand pixels — taller than the main column now that the sessions list collapses, so the booked state has a short left side and a long right one. More to the point, "Standing slot" is a power feature shown to somebody who has not completed a session yet, and "Your partner" repeats a name the band above has already said when you only have one.

If you want my opinion

A, and leave the length alone

A fixes a real thing — offering a standing slot to someone with no sessions is selling a habit to a person who has not started — and it costs two conditions. B is tidier and I do not think it is right: the plan is the only part of this page about the subject rather than the scheduling, and it should not become a line inside a streak card. The short-left long-right imbalance is worth living with; it is the honest shape of a page whose main column is empty because you have nothing booked, and the fix for that is having something booked.

Say which way and I will build it. Both are small; A is about twenty lines in app.html and no new CSS.
